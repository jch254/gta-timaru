## 1. How armed the police were (P1)

- Unarmed as the norm. Te Ara "Police" (https://teara.govt.nz/en/police; saved fetch/teara_police.txt): "Police in New Zealand have generally been unarmed – not carrying a firearm in normal circumstances – since the late 19th century. In 1964 the Armed Offenders Squad (AOS) was created to provide a specialist armed response unit."
- Firearms in cars: the universal lock box is 2012, not 1995. Wikipedia "New Zealand Police" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Police; saved): "Since 2012, frontline vehicles have had a locked box in the passenger foot-well containing two loaded and holstered Glock 17s and, in the rear of the vehicle, a locked case with two Bushmaster rifles and ballistic vests."
- Before that, some cars only. NZ Herald, 14 Jul 2010 (https://www.nzherald.co.nz/nz/hundreds-of-police-cars-routinely-carry-arms/23B4JZQIYV7KD742EHXCFT4FOI/): "641 of the 2700 or so police vehicles that might be used to carry firearms already have gun safes. They include pistol-only safes in dog handlers' vehicles, combined pistol and rifle safes in general-duties cars, bulk rifle and pistol safes in sergeants' vehicles and safes in rural officers' vehicles."
- A retired officer's recollection (opinion column, not official), Whanganui Chronicle 15 Oct 2019 (https://www.nzherald.co.nz/whanganui-chronicle/news/best-of-2019-rob-rattenbury-i-was-shot-at-twice-its-time-we-armed-our-police-officers/VEELRXV5OQW2SFIESG7ZJTMKEM/): "Since the late 80s there have been facilities in patrol cars for all general duties staff to carry firstly revolvers and then pistols and, in the past decade or so, Bushmaster rifles securely whilst working." Also: "Back in the late 70s and early 80s as a young detective and sergeant I routinely and daily carried a .38 revolver concealed under my jacket".
  Reading for 1995: revolver era; a firearm might be locked in a sergeant's, rural or dog car; a beat constable has none on the body.
- Baton. Te Papa (https://collections.tepapa.govt.nz/object/66823): "The Monadnock PR 24 baton was introduced by the New Zealand Police in the early 1980s for riot control. It replaced the previous shorter baton." Te Ara "Police": "Constables also carried batons (as they had since 1840)".
- NOT in 1995: Tasers (Wikipedia "New Zealand Police": "Tasers were first trialled in 2006 and in 2010 were rolled out"); stab vests (same page: "In 2006 the New Zealand Police introduced stab-resistant vests"); Bushmaster rifles (chosen 2005, see Unverified).
- Armed Offenders Squad. NZ Police (https://www.police.govt.nz/about-us/structure/police-teams-and-units/armed-offenders-squads): "These squads were first established in 1964 after the fatal shooting of four police officers"; "The basic methods of operating are to cordon, contain and appeal to armed offenders. These tactics are successful in the vast majority of incidents, which are resolved without the use of force."; "AOS members are all volunteers ... They are part-time, drawn from all branches of Police and operate on a call out basis."
- AOS call-out mechanics and numbers. Wikipedia "Armed Offenders Squad" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Offenders_Squad; saved): "the on-duty officers will be paged by the communications centre. They then assemble at their base, to draw arms and get other equipment, before responding to the scene."; "Between 1996 and 2009, the AOS conducted an average of 513 deployments a year."; "On average, shots are only fired once by the AOS for every 260 deployments."; "Squad sizes range from between 12 and 30 members and the largest are based in Auckland, Wellington and Christchurch." The kit list on that page (Glock 17, Bushmaster, Remington 870, Kevlar helmets, ballistic shields, Nissan Patrol 4WDs) is the modern kit; not dated to 1995.
- Dogs. Te Ara "Police": "Dogs were trained to assist with policing from 1956."
- Air support: none in Christchurch. Wikipedia "New Zealand Police": "The Air Support Unit, commonly known as its callsign Eagle, is based in Auckland ... Starting operation in 1988"; "In February 2020, an Eagle helicopter was based in Christchurch at Christchurch Airport for a five-week trial that was eventually cancelled".
- Shootings by police, statistics only. Te Ara "Police": "From 1941 to August 2011, 25 people were fatally shot by police". RNZ, 3 Aug 2015 (https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/national/280368/police-shootings-number-29-in-last-65-years; saved): "Since 1941, 29 people have been fatally shot by police". That is well under one a year nationally. FLAG: the RNZ page names the dead.

## 3. Traffic enforcement (P1)

- Merger. Wikipedia "Traffic Safety Service" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_Safety_Service; saved): "On 1 July 1992 the Traffic Safety Service was merged into the New Zealand Police ... Traffic Officers were gradually trained for other policing duties, with the majority eventually becoming sworn police officers ... A few elected to remain as non-sworn traffic officers but still employed by the Police." Also: "Traffic officers did not take an oath and consequently did not have the broad powers of arrest of a Police Constable".
- Names. Te Ara "Police": "the Ministry of Transport's Traffic Safety Service (once known as 'traffic cops'), which integrated into the New Zealand Police in 1992. This amalgamation aroused fears that traffic policing duties would damage relations between police and public."
- Why. Te Ara "Road accidents" p.3 (https://teara.govt.nz/en/road-accidents/page-3): "Cost-cutting was one reason for the merger ... research showed that many serious traffic offenders had other criminal convictions".
- Speed cameras. Same page: "in 1993 mobile speed cameras were introduced. These photographed the registration numbers of speeding drivers, and infringement notices were mailed to them."
- Breath testing. Same page: "Random stopping of drivers for breath tests started in 1984 ... In 1993 compulsory breath testing of all drivers passing mobile police checkpoints began, and 'booze buses' parked there speeded up the testing process."
- TV ads. Same page: "From the 1970s hard-hitting television advertisements began."
- Highway Patrol: SOURCES DISAGREE. Te Ara (same page): "In 1992, as the Traffic Safety Service, it joined with the Police, and a separate Police Highway Patrol was formed." Government release 15 Dec 2000 (https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/PA0012/S00268.htm): "Minister of Police Hon. George Hawkins has launched the highly visible Highway Patrol. The Highway Patrol will specialise on policing New Zealand highways and roads."; "New livery for Police Highway Patrol vehicles". Safe choice for 1995: no branded Highway Patrol cars; call them traffic police or (colloquially, by habit) traffic cops.
- Road toll. NZ Herald (https://www.nzherald.co.nz/nz/more-than-400-killed-on-roads-in-2005/SUCMUGXQS4ZLY7DVNFDJI5QAHM/): "1994 - 580", "1995 - 582". Te Ara "Road accidents": "The road toll and injury rate remained relatively high until the mid-1990s, when it began to decline markedly."
- Speeding ticket cost. Transport Amendment Act 1988, Second Schedule (https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1988/139/en/latest/): "Not more than 10 kilometres an hour 30 / More than 10 kilometres an hour but not more than 15 kilometres an hour 60 / ... not more than 20 ... 90 / ... not more than 25 ... 120 / ... not more than 30 ... 160 / ... not more than 35 ... 250 / ... not more than 40 ... 300". This is the 1988 scale; whether it was amended before 1995 was not checked.
- Later boy-racer laws, NOT for 1995. Wikipedia "Boy racer" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boy_racer; saved): "The Land Transport (Unauthorised Street and Drag Racing) Amendment Act 2003"; "In 2009 ... allow police to confiscate and 'crush' ... vehicles on the third offence within four years, issue infringements for 'cruising'".
- Pursuits: no 1995 policy text found (see Unverified).

## 5. Real kinds of racket (types only)

### 5a. Fisheries
- QMS. Te Ara "Fishing industry" (https://teara.govt.nz/en/fishing-industry; saved): "In October 1986, after two years of consultation and planning, the Quota Management System was introduced".
- Enforcer. Wikipedia "Ministry of Fisheries (New Zealand)" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Fisheries_(New_Zealand); saved): "The Ministry of Fisheries was established as a stand-alone agency in 1995 ... Previously, responsibility for fisheries belonged to [MAF]". So early 1995 = MAF Fisheries; later 1995 = Ministry of Fisheries ("MFish").
- Black market exists; fishery officers work with police. Te Ara video caption (https://teara.govt.nz/en/video/5118/paua-poaching): "Illegal harvesting of shellfish and crayfish is a serious problem in New Zealand. Because there is a black market for pāua, the Ministry of Fisheries undertakes operations to catch poachers. In this video clip, Fisheries and police officers collect and weigh bags of pāua hidden in a suburban garage."
- How black-market crayfish works (later Canterbury example of the type). MAF release 19 Mar 2012 (https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/PO1203/S00230/fishing-vessel-seized-in-canterbury.htm): "Fishery Officers in Canterbury have seized a commercial fishing vessel, a motor vehicle and 60 rock lobster pots following a four month surveillance operation into black market sales of rock lobster. Commercial fishers are required under the Fisheries Act to land their catch to a Licensed Fish Receiver (LFR)." The racket is catch that bypasses the licensed receiver and is sold for cash.
- Door-to-door sales and vehicle seizure. MFish release Jan 2006 (https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/PO0601/S00014/seizure-of-rock-lobster-and-paua.htm): "the illegal door to door sales of rock lobster for up to $15.00 each. If you are caught selling rock lobster and paua illegally Fishery Officers can seize any vehicle used in the commission of the offence." (2006 law; 1995 powers under the Fisheries Act 1983 not opened.)
- Customary take in 1995. Government release 6 Apr 2004 (https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/PA0404/S00110.htm): "Most customary fishing is currently done under Regulation 27 of the Fisheries (Amateur Fishing) Regulations 1986, which allow any group identifying itself as tangata whenua of an area to appoint people to authorise the taking of fish for hui or tangi."; "the Kaimoana Customary Fishing Regulations, which have been developed in accordance with the 1992 Maori Fisheries Settlement". Te Ara "Fishing industry": "Sealord deal, marked full and final settlement of Māori" (sentence truncated in the saved copy). For the game: in 1995 customary take is lawful, non-commercial, for a hui or tangi, on a written authorisation from a person appointed by the marae or rūnanga. It is not poaching and should never be written as a loophole used by the villains. The separate South Island customary regulations came later (see Unverified for the year).

### 5b. Stock theft
- Mechanism and identification (modern source, same method). RNZ, Sept 2022 (https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/top/474168/rustling-in-the-spotlight-after-hundreds-of-sheep-stolen-from-north-canterbury-farm): "it would have taken two or three trucks to move 500 lambs"; "quite a logistical exercise to organise the people, the dogs, the trucks, to get them through a yard and onto the trucks"; "vast majority are small numbers that go essentially into the food chain"; "lambs had ear markings specific to the farm they were born on, and cattle had electronic ear tags" (electronic tags are modern, not 1995); "if they're in the form of steak or roast lamb it's pretty hard to track them down". FLAG: names a real farmer; use method only.
- Law. NZ Herald (https://www.nzherald.co.nz/the-country/news/tougher-laws-for-cattle-rustling-but-more-police-needed-to-catch-culprits/QNT47YEQTFTDFVDKSNZY6IKEME/): theft of "livestock or any other animal, including beehives and farm dogs, an offence liable for up to seven years in prison" is a recent amendment, so no special rustling offence in 1995 (general theft law applied).

### 5e. Drugs
- Te Ara "Drugs" (https://teara.govt.nz/en/drugs/print): "the drug was overwhelmingly produced within New Zealand. - LSD: "There was some revival of use in the 1990s".
- Methamphetamine: Te Ara says "'P' took hold in the 1990s, largely replacing low-potency amphetamine sulfate ('speed')". So no P in a 1995 story; "speed" is the period stimulant.

## 7. Courts and consequences

- Sentencing ethos. Te Ara "Dishonesty crime": "efforts were made by the police and courts to keep young people out of jail, through the use of fines, probation and periodic detention."; "about 30% of those convicted for stealing cars go to prison" and "only 6% of those convicted of theft are imprisoned" (neither figure dated to 1995).
- Periodic detention. Te Ara "Prisons" (https://teara.govt.nz/en/prisons; saved): "periodic detention (community-based sentences involving supervised work at a work centre)". The same page mentions later change through "the Sentencing Act 2002"; that periodic detention ("PD") was still being imposed in 1995 is an inference, not a quoted fact.
- Prisons. Te Ara "Prisons": "Paparua: general prison for the South Island"; "A new women's prison was opened at Paparua, near Christchurch, in 1974."; "The women's prison at Addington was closed in 1950."
