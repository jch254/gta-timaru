# 10 Slang, insults and banter: how Canterbury talked in 1995, and lines for the game

Research notes, then draft lines. Each research item gives a source URL and a short quote seen on that page in this session. Anything not confirmed on a page I opened is marked **(unverified)**. Where an item is already sourced in another research file, that file is cited instead of fetching again. All the draft lines are original, invented for the game, and use no real person's name and no real brand (generic words like dairy, ute and jandals excepted).

Access this session: Te Aka Māori Dictionary, Wikipedia, Wiktionary and Te Ara all opened. newzealand.com refused (HTTP 403). Te Aka searches for two-word phrases ("e hoa", "e kō") returned "We couldn't find that", so those were checked through the headwords (hoa, kō) instead. Several Wiktionary idiom pages do not exist ("rough as guts", "away laughing", "plastic Maori").

## 1. NZ English insults and put-downs

Register scale: affectionate < mild < rude < very rude. "1995?" means: was it in everyday use in Canterbury by 1995?

### 1a. Confirmed on a page

- **bogan**: an unrefined, unsophisticated person; in NZ often a big-Aussie-V8 owner. Mild to rude, class-based. 1995: yes. Shared with Australia. Already sourced in [file 05](05-culture-and-daily-life.md), section 6b (Wikipedia "Bogan": "a person whose speech, clothing, behaviour, or attitudes are considered unrefined or unsophisticated"; Wikipedia "Boy racer": "often used in New Zealand in reference to owners of larger Australian cars, like Ford Falcons or Holden Commodores"). "Westie" is the Auckland word and would sound North Island.
- **sook**: a crybaby, whinger, wimp; also "have a sook" (a sulk). Mild, often affectionate. 1995: yes (dated "From 1933"). Australia, Atlantic Canada, New Zealand. Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sook : "(Australia, Atlantic Canada, New Zealand, slang, derogatory) A crybaby, a complainer, a whinger; a shy or timid person, a wimp; a coward." / "Don't be such a sook." The te reo equivalent is **tangiweto** (section 3).
- **piker**: someone who pulls out, goes home early, or backs out of a promise. Mild, affectionate. 1995: yes ("[from 20th c.]"). Australia and NZ. Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/piker : "(Australia, New Zealand, slang) One who refuses to go out with friends, or leaves a party early; a spoilsport or 'chicken'." / "Mate, don't be a piker!"
- **dag**: in NZ, a funny, eccentric person, a "hard case" (the existing barks use "Bit of a dag, your mate."); in Australia, an unfashionable dweeb. Also literally the dung-matted wool on a sheep's backside, which every Canterbury farm kid knows. Affectionate. 1995: yes. Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/dag : "(Australia slang, New Zealand, obsolete) An odd or eccentric person; someone who is a bit strange but amusingly so." and "A dangling lock of sheep's wool matted with dung", with NZ quotes from 1998 and 1999 (Massey Wool Association; NZ Grassland Association). Wiktionary labels the NZ person-sense "obsolete" today, which fits a 1995 setting. "Rattle your dags" (hurry up) **(unverified)**.
- **ratbag**: a rogue; in AU/NZ often an endearing word for a cheeky kid. Mild to affectionate. 1995: yes (1986 quote). Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ratbag : "(Australia, New Zealand, slang, sometimes endearing) A mischievous person, especially a child."
- **dole bludger**: an unemployed person accused of not looking for work. Rude, and politically loaded in 1995 (see the warning in section 3d). 1995: yes (1981 and 1993 quotes, Australian). Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/dole_bludger : "(Australia, New Zealand, derogatory, slang) An unemployed person who has no intention of seeking a job, and survives on government-funded unemployment benefits." Wikipedia's "Bludger" page calls bludger "Australian slang for a lazy person" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bludger).
- **cow cocky**: a small dairy farmer. Mild, teasing, rural. 1995: yes. Australia and NZ. Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/cow_cocky : "(Australia, New Zealand, informal) A small-scale dairy farmer." (Canterbury in 1995 was still mostly sheep and crop country, so "cow cocky" is an outsider's jibe there.)
- **hoon**: someone who drives recklessly to show off: burnouts, doughnuts, speeding. Mild to rude. 1995: yes, but sliding: Te Ara says "boy racers" was the 1990s term and "hoons" the 1970s-80s one (file 05, early section). Australia and NZ. Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoon : "an Australian and New Zealand term describing a person who deliberately drives a vehicle in a reckless or dangerous manner" / "Hoon activities (or hooning) can include speeding, burnouts, doughnuts, or screeching tyres." So older peds say hoon, younger ones say boy racer.
- **JAFA**: an Aucklander. Rude (contains the f-word), regional. Te Ara, "English language in New Zealand", https://teara.govt.nz/en/english-language-in-new-zealand/print : "acronyms such as ... JAFA (just another fucking Aucklander)", listed among abbreviations made "Since the late 20th century". Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JAFA : "In 1999 the Tourism Auckland organisation launched a 'Jafa' advertising campaign." / "Aucklanders initially adopted the word ironically." Use in 1995: probable (a 1999 campaign could only play on a word already known), but no 1995 citation was found **(unverified for 1995)**. Related, same page: "'New Zealand stops (or starts) at the Bombay Hills'", "'Queen Street Farmer' – Urban businessman with rural investments", "'Remuera Tractor' – An SUV vehicle", and the older "'Rangitoto Yanks'".
- **plonker**: a fool. Mild. Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/plonker : "(UK, Ireland, Commonwealth, slang, derogatory) A fool." Flag: **mainly British**. Understood in NZ; whether it was common in Canterbury in 1995 **(unverified)**. Use sparingly, perhaps from an older English-born ped.
- **muppet**: an incompetent or foolish person. Mild. Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/muppet : "(UK, Ireland, Commonwealth, slang, derogatory) An incompetent or foolish person.", earliest quote "1996 April 18". Flag: **mainly British**, and the earliest quote is 1996. The existing barks already use "ya muppet"; it is borderline for 1995 **(unverified)**.
- **dropkick**: a loser, a contemptible person. Rude. Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/drop_kick : "(Australia, slang, derogatory) An insignificant, contemptible or unfashionable person; a loser", quote from 1989. Flag: **Australian**; NZ use in 1995 **(unverified)**, though Australian TV and rugby league carried a lot of Aussie slang across the Tasman.
- **munter / munted**: Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/munter : "Late 1990s", "(UK, Ireland, slang, derogatory) An ugly person" and "(Commonwealth, slang) A person often impaired by alcohol or recreational drugs." Wikipedia's NZ English glossary has "munted (adj.) – broken; ruined; wrecked" (file 05, 6b). Te Ara says New Zealanders "connected the existing terms munted (broken) and muntage (destruction) with the devastation of the quakes" of 2010 to 2011 (Te Ara page above). Flag: **probably later than 1995**. Avoid "munter"; use "munted" rarely if at all.
- **bugger**, **Pommy git**, **sheep shagger**: Te Ara (page above): "The Broadcasting Standards and Advertising Standards authorities determine what New Zealand terms are acceptable for use in the media. Pommy git, sheep shagger and bugger have all been ruled acceptable." Bugger: mild, everywhere, every age. Pommy (an English person) is Australian in origin (Wikipedia NZ English: "pom or pommy (an Englishman)"); mild ribbing. Sheep shagger: rude; the jibe Australians throw at New Zealanders, so a Kiwi can throw it back across the Tasman or at himself.
- **howlybag** (a whinger) and **crook**: Te Ara (page above): "New Zealand slang terms include howlybag (whinger)"; "Crook has many meanings in New Zealand English, including angry, bad, broken, inadequate, empty, ill ... If a person is as crook as a dog, she or he is really ill." Dates for howlybag **(unverified)**.
- **Australian-origin words that are fully at home in NZ**: Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_English : "New Zealand English terms of Australian origin include bushed (lost or bewildered), chunder (to vomit) ... drongo (a foolish or stupid person) ... larrikin (mischievous person) ... skite (verb: to boast) ... wowser (non-drinker of alcohol, or killjoy), and ute". All fine for 1995. (The same list's "cooker", for a conspiracy theorist, is a 2020s word: do not use.)

### 1b. Not confirmed on a page (in common knowledge only)

All **(unverified)**; listed so writers know the register.
- **egg**: a fool. Mild, very Kiwi ("ya egg"); already in the barks.
- **dick, dickhead, knob**: rude. Universal English; "knob" leans British.
- **nana** ("don't be a nana"): a silly person. Mild, affectionate. AU/NZ.
- **mongrel**: a nasty person, or a thing that won't work ("this mongrel of a motor"). Rude. Never tie it to the real gang with a similar name.
- **wally**: a fool. Mild. **British.**
- **clown**: mild, universal.
- **tosser, wanker**: rude. Both British-flavoured but common in NZ; wanker is ruder.
- **townie**: a town person, said by country people. Mild.
- **prick, arsehole, bastard, bloody, shit, fuck, piss off**: rude to very rude; all in everyday 1995 use. "Bloody" and "bastard" can be affectionate ("you lucky bastard").

### 1c. Too late for 1995 (do not use)

- **"chur"** (cheers, thanks): in the Wikipedia NZ English glossary ("chur (interj) — hello, cheers, thanks"), cited to a 2023 NZ Herald piece on new Oxford entries. Its first use is not dated on any page I opened, but it reads as 2000s **(unverified date)**. Owner's rule: out.
- **"Yeah right"** as a billboard punchline: Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tui_(beer) : "The billboard campaign lasted from 1997 until 2016." Two years too late. Plain "yeah, right" as sarcasm is fine; the billboard formula ("X. Yeah right.") is not.
- **"nek minnit"**: Te Ara (page above): "In 2011 a short clip posted to the video-sharing website YouTube propelled the New Zealand slang term 'nek minnit' (next minute) into linguistic stardom".
- **"sus"** in the teenage "that's sus" sense: a recent revival **(unverified date)**. Out.
- **"munter"**: late 1990s (above). **"hardout"** (Wikipedia NZE glossary): date **(unverified)**, sounds 2000s; avoid.
- **"cooker"**: 2020s (above).

## 2. Comic Kiwi similes and set phrases

### 2a. Confirmed on a page

- **box of birds** (feeling great): New Zealand. Te Ara (page above): "in no other nation do people feel a box of birds (in a good mood)". Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/box_of_birds : "(New Zealand, dated)". Dated now; perfect for 1995.
- **up the boohai** (hopelessly lost, out in the back of beyond): New Zealand. Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/up_the_boohai : "(New Zealand, informal, idiomatic) Utterly and completely lost; lost in a remote location." Etymology "Possibly from Puhoi." Wikipedia NZ English: the 1979 Heinemann dictionary dated "boohai" to "(1920)".
- **the wop-wops** (the middle of nowhere): New Zealand. Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/wop-wops : "(New Zealand) An out of the way place; the boondocks or backcountry.", quote from 1979: "things could happen out here in the wop-wops."
- **pack a sad** (to sulk; or of a machine, to break down): New Zealand. Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/pack_a_sad : "(New Zealand, colloquial) To break down, to stop working." / "To be negative or depressed." First date **(unverified)**.
- **spit the dummy** (throw a childish tantrum): Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/spit_the_dummy : "(informal, idiomatic, chiefly Australia) To overreact (as an adult) to a situation, in an angry and childish manner." Flag: **Australian**, widely used in NZ.
- **mad as a meat axe** (furious, or wild): Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/mad_as_a_meat_axe : "(Australia, simile, colloquial) Very irate; crazy with rage." Flag: **Australian**; a freezing worker would love it.
- **she'll be right**: Wikipedia NZ English: "The most common use of this is in the phrase 'She'll be right' meaning either 'It will be okay' or 'It is close enough to what is required'."
- **crook as a dog** (really ill): Te Ara, quoted in 1a.
- **tiki tour** (a roundabout drive, a look around): Te Ara: "Tiki tour means a look-around, drive or trip (and was originally derived from an old New Zealand tourist company of the same name)."
- **smoko** (tea break): Te Ara: Bennett in the 1940s listed "smoke-oh (or smoko, a tea break for workers)" as distinctive. (File 05 had smoko unverified; this confirms it.)
- **good-o**, **going eyes out** (going flat out), **no fear** (certainly not): Te Ara: "One example is good-o."; "'no fear' (certainly not) and 'going eyes out' (going fast), were heard in the next century."
- **Canterbury weather words**: Te Ara's list of weather terms includes "nor'west arch", "southerly buster", "Hughie" (as in "send her down, Hughie" for rain), "Māori summer" (a warm spell late in the season) and "dirty weather".
- **the Shield** as "the log o' wood": Te Ara: "Rugby's Ranfurly Shield is commonly known as the log o' wood, the old log, or simply The Shield."
- **tinny** (cannabis in tinfoil): Te Ara: "a tinny is cannabis wrapped in tinfoil and a tinny house is a place where cannabis is purchased."
- **car conversion** (car theft): Wikipedia NZ English: "convert (verb) – to steal a car, hence also the name for car theft being car conversion". Ideal for theft barks.
- **jug** (the electric kettle) and **chilly bin**: Wikipedia NZ English: "jug (noun) – a kettle"; "chilly bin | Esky".
- **puckerooed** (broken), from te reo pakaru: Te Ara: "puckerooed (broken, from the Māori word 'pakaru')".
- **sweet as**, **dairy**, **durry**, **eh?**: Wikipedia NZ English glossary (file 05, 6b).

## 3. Everyday te reo Māori in Kiwi banter

Every headword below was opened on Te Aka (https://maoridictionary.co.nz/search?keywords=WORD) unless marked. Macrons are as Te Aka prints them. Register uses the same scale as section 1. "Anyone?" says whether any ped (Pākehā, Māori, Pasifika, any age) can say it in the game.

### 3a. Teasing, put-downs and mild swearing

- **hōhā**: "be boring, tiresome, bored, wearisome, fed up with ... annoying"; as a noun "nuisance, bother, bore, hassle, pain in the neck". Te Aka gives the idiom **hōhā tahi** "what a nuisance, how annoying", with the example translated "You're a bloody nuisance!" Mild. Line: "Hōhā, this queue." / "You're hōhā, you." Anyone: yes. One of the words Pākehā Cantabrians really did use in 1995 (my inference; printed "hoha").
- **pōrangi**: "be insane, mad, crazy, mentally ill, deranged, beside oneself, headstrong, hurried, stupid". Mild. Line: "That's pōrangi, bro. Don't." Anyone: yes, but only about wild behaviour, never about someone's mental health.
- **kūware**: "ignorant, unaware, unenlightened, naive, uninformed ... low in the social scale, held in no esteem" (variant **kūare**). Rude-ish, because of the "low in the social scale" sense. Line: "Don't be kūware, read the sign." Anyone: no. Keep it for Māori characters ribbing their own whānau; from a Pākehā stranger it reads as a class put-down.
- **pōhēhē**: "mistaken, in error, confused, deluded"; as a noun "mistake, error, misunderstanding, blunder". Te Aka has the idiom **tēnā pōhēhē tēnā!** "you're so mistaken, that's a load of rubbish, you're so wrong, yeah right". Mild. Line: "Pōhēhē, mate. Shield's not going anywhere." Anyone: yes. (Better for Māori voices; few Pākehā would have known it in 1995.)
- **tutū**: "to be stirred up ... mischievous, disobedient ... undisciplined". Wikipedia's loanword list adds the NZ English sense: "Used in New Zealand English to mean 'fidget' or 'fiddle' e.g. 'Don't tutū with that!'" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_English_words_of_M%C4%81ori_origin). Watch the macron: **tutu** without it is the poisonous shrub. Mild. Line: "Stop tutūing with the radio!" Anyone: yes.
- **tūtae**: "dung, excrement, shit, faeces, poo, droppings". Mild swear, the te reo "shit". Line: "Tūtae! Stood in it again." Anyone: yes. Te Aka also has **tūtae tara** "you idiot, what a fool, what a moron", but **avoid it**: tara also has a sexual-anatomy sense (not checked on Te Aka this session), and the owner has excluded the c-word, so treat it as off-limits.
- **hūpē**: "mucus from the nose, snot". Plus **ihu hūpē** "inexperienced person, novice, greenhorn" (literally snotty nose). Mild, affectionate. Line: "Wipe your hūpē, boy." / "He's ihu hūpē, first week on the job." Anyone: yes.
- **whakamā**: "to be ashamed, shy, bashful, embarrassed". Line: "Don't be whakamā, ask her." Anyone: yes; it is a real feeling, not an insult, so tease gently.
- **pakaru**: (stative) "be smashed, shattered, broken, broken down". No macron in Te Aka (Wikipedia's list prints "pakarū"; follow Te Aka). The anglicised "puckerooed" is in Te Ara. Idioms on the same Te Aka page: **waha pakaru** "braggart, boaster ... loud-mouth"; **pakaru te pūkoro** "broke, having no money" ("Kua pakaru taku pūkoro. / I'm broke."); **pakaru mai te haunga** "pathetic, useless, it's the pits"; **pakaru (ana) ngā hamuti** "shit-scared, scared shitless". Mild (hamuti one is rude). Line: "Car's pakaru. So am I." / "Shut up, waha pakaru." Anyone: yes.
- **pōhara**: "(loan) to be poor, poverty-stricken, broke, hard up". Te Aka marks it a loanword. Mild. Line: "Can't, bro. Pōhara till Thursday." Anyone: yes, about yourself. Never aim it at a Māori or Pasifika character as a jibe about being poor.
- **puku**: "stomach, abdomen ... belly, tummy" (Wikipedia list: "belly, usually a big one"). Idioms on Te Aka: **te puku** "lying, full of nonsense" (example translated "You're all bullshit."); **puku te rae** "angry, annoyed, livid"; **puku kau anō** "what a cheek you've got" (example ends "Puku kau anō, e kō!"); **ngau te puku** "to have a stomach-ache" (example: "I've got a stomach ache because I was a guts."). Affectionate. Line: "Look at the puku on him." Anyone: yes. One of the commonest te reo words in Pākehā mouths in 1995 (my inference).
- **ihu**: "nose, snout". Idioms: **ihu kurī** "nosy parker, busybody"; **tō ihu** "don't be nosy! butt out!"; **ihu tū** "snob". Mild. Line: "Tō ihu, e hoa." / "Bit of an ihu kurī, aren't ya?" Anyone: yes. Perfect for the chat bark when the player stands and stares.
- **taringa**: "ear"; also "to be deaf, unhearing, indifferent". Idioms: **taringa pākura** "stubborn, obstinate"; **taringa mārō** "inattentive, deaf, stubborn"; **patu taringa** "tell a fib ... pull someone's leg" (Te Aka's own example: "Tau said you won the lotto. ... He's pulling your leg."); **tō taringa** "listen more carefully!". Mild. Line: "Patu taringa, bro. You never won." Anyone: yes.
- **kanohi**: "face, countenance"; "eye, eyesight". Idioms: **kanohi taiaha** "two-faced person"; **kanohi kitea** "seen face, physical presence" (being seen at things, tangihanga especially: respectful, not a joke). Line: "Kanohi taiaha, that one." Anyone: yes (the insult); keep kanohi kitea serious.
- **raruraru**: "trouble, problem, dispute, conflict"; also "debt". Te Aka idiom: "he raruraru kei te haere" "trouble is brewing". Line: "No raruraru, e hoa. All good." Anyone: yes.
- **tangiweto**: "crybaby, sook, sooky-bubba", with a Māori-language citation from 1994 (HM 4/1994). Affectionate. Line: "Don't be a tangiweto, it's a scratch." Anyone: yes.

### 3b. Everyday words, calls and exclamations

- **taihoa**: "by and by, wait, don't ... yet"; also **taihoa [koe] i a [au]!** "I'll get you! I'll sort you out!" Wikipedia list: "taihoa – not yet, wait a while". Line: "Taihoa! I'm crossing!" Anyone: yes; very common in 1995.
- **kia tūpato**: Te Aka headword **tūpato** "to be cautious, careful, wary". Note the macron is on the u only. Line: "Kia tūpato, it's icy." Anyone: yes.
- **auē**: "heck! oh dear! - expression of astonishment or distress"; **aue** (no macrons) "heck! - expression of surprise"; **auē taukuri ē** "oh dear! for crying out loud!". The house test already bans "Aue!" for "Auē!". Anyone: yes. Note: Samoan has a near-identical cry, so it suits Pasifika voices too (my inference).
- **e tama / e hoa mā / e hika / e koro**: Te Aka idiom "e [tama] (mā) (e)": "good heavens boy! good heavens! far out! for goodness sake! ... used with terms of address (e.g. tama, kui, tama, hine, koro, hoa, hika, etc.)". Example: "E hoa mā e!" translated "Good heavens!" Anyone: yes, "e hoa" from anyone; "e tama" mostly from Māori speakers to boys and young men.
- **hoa**: "friend, companion, mate, partner"; term of address "E hoa mā, kia kamakama ... Hey guys, hurry up". Anyone: yes.
- **kō**: "girl, younger woman, babe, darling - used in addressing girls and young women and is short for kōtiro". So **e kō**. Anyone: Māori aunties and nannies, mostly; from a strange man to a young woman it would sound like a pickup line.
- **koro**: "elderly man, grandfather, grandad, grandpa - term of address to an older man". Anyone: yes.
- **kuia**: "elderly woman, grandmother, female elder". Anyone: yes, respectfully. Teasing a kuia is the kuia's privilege, not the player's.
- **kaumātua**: "adult, elder ... a person of status within the whānau". Anyone: yes, said with respect. Never the butt of a joke.
- **mokopuna / moko**: mokopuna "grandchildren, grandchild"; moko "grandchild - a term of address used by an older person for a grandchild or a young child", example "He aha hei parakuihi māu, e moko?" Anyone: older peds. (Do not confuse with moko, facial tattoo.)
- **pōua** and **tāua** (Kāi Tahu words for grandfather and grandmother): pōua "old person, elderly man"; "grandfather", with Te Aka's example "ngā pōua me ngā tāua o Ngāi Tahu". (File 07 found "poua and taua" in Te Karaka, 1996.) Ideal for Aunty Rena's side of the family instead of the northern koro and kui. Anyone: Ngāi Tahu characters.
- **kai, whānau, mahi, kaupapa, ka pai, aroha, kia ora, kia kaha, ka kite anō, haere rā, tēnā koe**: Wikipedia's loanword list: "kai: food", "whānau: extended family", "mahi: work, employment", "kaupapa: agenda, policy or principle", "kapai: very pleasant; good, fine. From Māori 'ka pai'", "aroha: Love, sympathy, affection". Wikipedia NZ English: "kai ... used by Māori and Pākehā alike"; "Kia ora ... can also mean 'thank you'"; "tēnā koe"; "haere rā"; "kia kaha"; "Ka kite anō ('until I see you again') is quite commonly used." The Te Aka entries for these were not opened this session. **Aroha mai** (sorry) and **Mōrena** follow house style and the existing test, not a page opened this session **(unverified on Te Aka)**.
- **bro, cuz, nan, aunty**: English, used across Māori, Pasifika and Pākehā youth in 1995 **(unverified)**. "Aunty" for any older woman of your community is respectful.
- Pasifika: **talofa** (Samoan "hello"): Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/talofa : "Samoan ... hello", citing Milner's 1993 Samoan Dictionary. Other Samoan or Tongan words were not checked; keep Pasifika peds mostly in Kiwi English with talofa, auē, bro, aunty and family, church and rugby as everyday life, not as gags.

### 3c. Southern dialect, and how Pākehā South Islanders actually used te reo in 1995

- **k for ng** (Kāi Tahu / Ngāi Tahu): Wikipedia "Māori language" (file 05, 6b): "In South Island dialects, ng merged into k in many regions. Thus Kāi Tahu and Ngāi Tahu are variations in the name of the same iwi". Examples: "nohoka (nohoanga ...), tikaka (tikanga ...) ... whaka (whanga, harbour)". Ōtākou Rūnaka (file 07, B3): early Ngāi Tahu writers "spoke with 'k' but were taught with the 'ng'."
- **What 1995 print looked like** (file 07, B3, from 1996 Te Karaka and the 1996 Act): no macrons; "Ngai Tahu" far more than "Kai Tahu"; "runanga" not "rūnaka"; k forms fixed mainly in hapū and place names (Kati Huirapa, Aoraki). Wikipedia list: "Since about 2015, macrons have rapidly become standard usage". So a 1995 sign or newspaper in the game would print "hoha", "Maori", "whanau". Spoken lines (subtitles) use the macrons, as the house test requires.
- **How much Pākehā Cantabrians used** (inference; no page states it for 1995 Canterbury): Wikipedia NZ English says "Māori speakers of NZE use Māori loanwords more often than non-Māori speakers" and the loan of Māori words "has increased since the 1990s" (file 05). Te Ara: few Māori words entered NZ English between the 1860s and "around 1970", then borrowing resumed. So in 1995: a Pākehā ped in Timaru or Christchurch says kia ora, ka pai, kai, puku, hōhā, taihoa, puckerooed, whānau now and then, haere mai, and the odd "e hoa". Māori peds, and Pākehā married into Māori whānau, use the wider set: e tama, e kō, auē, tō ihu, whakamā, raruraru, kia tūpato, tangiweto, waha pakaru. Words like "mahi", "kaupapa" and "aroha mai" in casual Pākehā speech lean later; fine from Māori peds, rarer from Pākehā ones.
- **Ngāi Tahu words for the cast**: Rena's whānau are at Arowhenua (cast.ts). Let them say pōua and tāua, kāika (for kāinga), and "Kāi Tahu" in speech, while anything printed says "Ngai Tahu".

### 3d. What NOT to use, and why

These are excluded outright: no ped, no cast member, no radio voice, not even a villain. GTA-style crudeness is the house style, but the joke always lands on the speaker or on the situation, never on someone's race, sexuality or disability.

Slurs and put-downs aimed at Māori, Pasifika and Asian people:
- **"hori"**: Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/hori : "(New Zealand, slang, sometimes derogatory) A Māori." / "May be pejorative when used by non-Māori." It is a racial slur from outsiders. Some Māori use it of themselves (Wiktionary quotes a 1983 novel), but that is theirs, not the game's to borrow. Watch out for a trap: Te Aka lists **hori / horihori** (false, lie) as a synonym in the patu taringa entry. It is a real te reo word, but it is spelt like the slur, so avoid that too.
- **"coconut"** (for Pacific people), **"bunga"**, **"FOB" / "fresh off the boat"**, **"plastic Māori"** (sneering at someone's Māori identity), **"half-caste"** (Te Aka's own example sentence translates "hāwhe-kāehe" as half-caste; it is historical, and an insult in a joke) and **"afakasi"** used as a label (Te Ara lists it as a Samoan word "given to groups of people in New Zealand"): all excluded because each attacks someone for their ethnicity or their claim to it. (Wiktionary entries for these were not opened; the exclusion does not depend on a dictionary label.)
- **"Jap"**, including "Jap import", which was everyday car talk in 1995. Excluded because it is an ethnic slur for Japanese people; say "import", "Japanese import" or "used import". Likewise "Chinaman", "chink", "slant-eyed", "gook", "Paki", "curry muncher", "Asian invasion", and any mock accent.
- **Racialised dole jokes**: in the year to March 1995, unemployment was 5.6% for Pākehā, 19.4% for Māori and 20.7% for the Pacific Island group (file 04, section 2, Y96 Table 14.10). In April 1995 a government whip resigned after impersonating "a Maori unemployment beneficiary, 'Hone'" on talkback radio (file 04, section 5). That is exactly the joke the game must not make. "Dole bludger" can be aimed at Mack, at the speaker, or at a Pākehā ped; never at a Māori or Pasifika character, and never with a Māori name or accent.
- **"Māori time" / "Island time"** gags, and "Mowry"-style mispronunciation as a punchline.

Homophobic terms:
- **poofter, poof, nancy boy, faggot, fag, homo, queer** (as an insult), and **"that's so gay"**. Wikipedia NZ English records "faggot or fag (interchangeable with the British poof and poofter)" as part of the NZ word stock, so they were current in 1995, which is why the ban has to be explicit. Excluded because they attack sexuality. **Takatāpui** is a te reo word for queer Māori identity; it must never be used as an insult or a joke.

Ableist terms:
- **retard, spaz, mong** (note: mong is not mongrel), **psycho, schizo**, "window licker", "special" as a sneer. Excluded because they make disability the joke. "Pōrangi" (3a) is allowed only about behaviour ("that's pōrangi"), never about mental illness, and "a sandwich short of a picnic" style lines should be about one bad decision, not a person's intellect.

The c-word:
- Excluded by the owner, in English and in disguise ("you see you next Tuesday" and the like). Also the te reo **tūtae tara** idiom, as a precaution (3a).

Mocking tikanga or te reo:
- No jokes about tapu, marae protocol, the hongi, karakia, tangihanga, moko (facial tattoo) or the haka as a gag. Marae grounds are already a no-panic, no-fight zone in the code (ped.ts, "Is this point inside a marae's grounds"). No fake te reo, no te reo spoken in a funny voice, no te reo as the punchline. The Māori words in this file are there because Kiwis of every colour use them with affection; the joke is in what is said, not in the language.

Also out: real gang names, real people's names (the 1995 cameos are look-alikes under invented names, cast.ts), and jokes about sexual violence.

## 4. 1995 touchstones for jokes, as types

The game invents its brands and people; these are the joke types, with the names left out. Dates come from [docs/RESEARCH.md](../RESEARCH.md) ("1995, in order") and [file 04](04-era-1990-1996.md).

- **The dole and the works**: the killing season "started around August and reached a peak in January", "the chain", tallies (file 04, section 1); seasonal men not called back, laid off "by letter" (Mack, cast.ts); benefits cut in the 1991 budget (file 04); "between seasons" as a euphemism; the dole office queue; living on the benefit till Thursday.
- **The phone box**: phonecards, a toll call to the North Island, reversing the charges, a box that eats coins and smells of wee (file 05, 6a: phonecards probable from 1989). No cellphones in everyday hands: a brick phone is a joke about a flash car dealer.
- **Saturday night's draw**: the TV lottery draw at about 8pm Saturday, the only draw of the week in 1995, and scratch cards (file 05, section 5). In lines say "the draw" or "the lottery": the real product names are brands.
- **The tinny** (Te Ara, above), the dairy, the bach (the crib from Timaru south; file 05, 6b), the jug, the chilly bin, the pie and a flavoured milk, the fish and chips on Friday.
- **Gear**: gumboots, the bush shirt (the woollen swanny pattern of the region, file 05: say "bush shirt" in lines), stubbies (short shorts; to an Australian, a beer bottle), jandals, walk shorts with long socks (file 05, section 3).
- **The laconic Southern Man**: the high-country bloke from a South Island beer's TV ads (file 05: "Speight's Southern man advertising campaign"; the start date is unverified in file 05, so treat it as an archetype, not an ad quote). Never in lines by name.
- **Rugby clubrooms and the Shield**: sausages, raffles, a jug of beer; Canterbury held the Shield until losing it 0-35 to Auckland on 23 September 1995 (RESEARCH.md). "The log o' wood" (Te Ara).
- **The A&P show** and **Show Week**: Show Day was Friday 10 November 1995, Canterbury's anniversary holiday (RESEARCH.md). Sheep, candy floss, the cup races.
- **Boy racers and imports**: 55.2% of 1995 car registrations were used imports (RESEARCH.md); old men grumble about Japanese imports, young men about Aussie V8 owners ("bogans").
- **The America's Cup**: won 5-0 on 13 May 1995 (San Diego), the red socks (RESEARCH.md). Peds who have never sailed are suddenly experts.
- **Mururoa**: France announced the tests on 13 June, the navy ship sailed 19 August, the first test was 5 September (RESEARCH.md). Boycotting French wine and cheese **(unverified as a 1995 Canterbury habit)**. Say "the French", never "frogs".
- **Ruapehu**: erupted 18 and 23 September (RESEARCH.md): "North Island problems."
- **The World Cup final**, lost 15-12 on 24 June (RESEARCH.md), and the national grudge about the boys being sick before it **(the food-poisoning story is folklore; do not name anyone)**.
- **MMP**: chosen in the 1993 referendum, first used on 12 October 1996 (file 04, section 5). In 1995 it is "next year" and "two votes".
- **Mainlanders against Aucklanders**: JAFAs, "New Zealand stops at the Bombay Hills", "Remuera tractors" (Wikipedia JAFA page).
- **Christchurch against Timaru**: the city's new tram (back on 4 February 1995, RESEARCH.md), one-way streets, the Square and its snobs; against the Bay, the port, the freezing works, "the place you drive through on the way to somewhere". Timaru folk call Christchurch "the big smoke" **(unverified)**; Christchurch folk say Timaru with a sigh.
- **Weather**: the nor'wester (hot, dry, everyone grumpy), the nor'west arch, the southerly buster and the 20-degree drop, the winter of heavy snow and lost lambs (RESEARCH.md: winter 1995, "about 50,000 lambs lost").

## Notes for writers

- Aim the joke at the speaker, the situation, the weather, the town, or the player. Swear at Mack all you like; never at anyone's race, sexuality or disability. Check every new line against section 3d before it goes in.
- Te reo should sound like it does in a Timaru chip shop: dropped into English, mid-sentence, with affection. It is never the punchline and never spoken in a "funny" voice. Spell it with Te Aka's macrons (hōhā, pōrangi, tūpato, tutū, pakaru without one) and copy them exactly; tutu without the macron is a poisonous shrub.
- Match the word to the voice: Pākehā peds mostly use kia ora, ka pai, kai, puku, hōhā, taihoa and puckerooed; Māori peds use the full set (e tama, e kō, tō ihu, whakamā, tangiweto, waha pakaru). Ngāi Tahu characters say pōua and tāua, and the southern k in speech; anything printed (signs, papers) drops the macrons and says "Ngai Tahu".
- Keep it 1995: no cellphones in everyday hands, no internet, no "chur", "sus", "nek minnit", or Tui-style "Yeah right" billboards, no 2020s words. Older peds say hoon, younger ones boy racer. Say "import", never "Jap import".
- Keep the barks date-free; anything about the Cup, the Shield, Mururoa, Ruapehu or the World Cup final belongs in a date-gated conversation (see Calendar flags).
- No real names, no real brands. "The draw" not the lottery's brand name; "bush shirt" not the brand; "a South Island beer ad" not the brewery; "the cops" and "the works" are fine. Generic Kiwi words (dairy, ute, jandals, bach, jug, chilly bin) are fine.
- Prefer the Kiwi and southern versions: crib from Timaru south, bach in Christchurch; "possum in the headlights", not deer; nor'wester and southerly, never "the wind".
- Mack is dry, self-deprecating and sweary, and his best jokes come from the works: the chain, smoko, the tally, the letter that didn't call him back. Keep his darkest lines comic and quick, then move on.
- Before shipping, run the length check: barks at most 48 characters (looks.test.ts enforces this), conversation lines and quips at most 60.
