### B3. Dialect, mahinga kai, register

- The k for ng convention. CONFIRMED as the marker of the dialect. Ōtākou Rūnaka, https://www.otakourunaka.co.nz/language-history : "The "k" replaces the "ng" and there are many examples of this particularly in place names."; early Ngāi Tahu writers "spoke with "k" but were taught with the "ng"."; "In 2000, the Kāi Tahu Māori language strategy, Kotahi Mano Kāika, Kotahi Mano Wawata was launched". Wikipedia Māori language: "In South Island dialects, ng merged into k in many regions."; "Since 2000, the government has altered the official names of several southern place names to the southern dialect forms by replacing ng with k."
- How widely k was written in 1995. No page states this directly. Evidence from two documents of 1996, read here:
  - Te Karaka, the Ngāi Tahu magazine, Raumati/Summer 1996 (saved working/tk/tk03.pdf and tk03.txt): no macrons; "Ngai Tahu" about 147 times against "Kai Tahu" about 15; "runanga" 32 times and "runaka" not at all; "mahinga kai (food-gathering) resources"; "kaumatua"; the words "taua" and "poua" for elders, glossed in the magazine's own translation as "the love of our poua and taua"; a Māori-language piece prints a marked k where standard Māori has ng (it comes through the PDF text layer as "ru~a", "Kai Tahuta~a").
  - Te Runanga o Ngai Tahu Act 1996 (saved working/leg1996.txt, legislation.govt.nz): "Ngai Tahu Whanui means the collective of the individuals who descend from the primary hapu of Waitaha, Ngati Mamoe, and Ngai Tahu, namely, Kati Kuri, Kati Irakehu, Kati Huirapa, Ngai Tuahuriri, and Kai Te Ruahikihiki."
  - Reading for the game (my inference from those two documents, not a sourced statement): in mid 1990s print the iwi was overwhelmingly "Ngai Tahu" without macron, "runanga" not "rūnaka", "mahinga kai" not "mahika kai"; k forms were fixed in hapū and place names (Kati Huirapa, Kati Kuri, Aoraki) and used by some writers in Māori-language text. Consistent macrons and k spelling (Kāi Tahu, rūnaka, kāika) look like later house style.
- Mahinga kai. CONFIRMED as food gathering places and resources, covering the species listed. Claim history page: "the tribe lost its access to its mahinga kai, or food gathering resources". Atlas: Ōtākaro foods "tuna (eel), inaka (whitebait), kōkopu (native trout), kanakana (lamprey), waikōura (freshwater crayfish), waikākahi (freshwater mussel)"; Ōpihi foods "tuna (eels), inaka (whitebait), kōkopu (native trout), upokororo (grayling), kanakana (lampreys), pātiki (flounder), aua (yellow-eyed mullet)"; Timaru "ika (fish), makō (shark), hāpuku (groper), pipi, pāua, kina, and kaeo"; tītī are "sooty shearwaters/'muttonbirds'" harvested on the Tītī Islands off Rakiura. Spelling notes from the atlas: inaka is the usual form (the Rangitata entry prints "inanga"); freshwater crayfish is waikōura (plain kōura was not checked); kāuru is cabbage tree root.
- Hui, kai for a hui, manaakitanga at a marae. PARTLY. The only directly relevant first-hand statement found: https://arowhenua.org/resources : "The Kāti Huirapa people were also renowned for their hospitality in accommodating guests". The 1996 Te Karaka uses "hui", "Hui-a-Tau" ("the 1995 Ngai Tahu Hui-a-Tau"), "karanga" ("The taua of Kaiapoi gave their karanga") and "kaumatua" in ordinary English sentences. No source was opened that sets out how to describe marae protocol or hui catering respectfully, so that guidance is STILL UNVERIFIED and should go to a Ngāi Tahu or Arowhenua reader.
- "Aunty", "e hoa", "ka pai", "kia kaha", "whānau", "tamariki", "mahi", "kai" in 1990s South Island Māori English. PARTLY. In the 1996 Te Karaka text: "Kia kaha." closes an English-language sports item; "kia ora", "whanau", "tamariki", "hui", "tangi", "taua", "poua" occur in the issue; "Aunty" occurs once; "e hoa" and "ka pai" do not occur in that issue. Wikipedia Rāpaki has "'Aunty' Doe Parata" for a respected weaver (not a 1990s source). So kia kaha, kia ora, whānau, tamariki, hui, tangi, taua and poua are attested in Ngāi Tahu writing of 1996; "Aunty" is weakly attested; "e hoa", "ka pai", and "mahi" and "kai" as stand-alone English-sentence words are STILL UNVERIFIED for this setting. "taua" and "poua" are worth knowing as the terms that magazine uses for elders.

### B4. The claim through 1995, and Arowhenua working life

Timeline (Te Rūnanga o Ngāi Tahu, "Claim History", https://ngaitahu.iwi.nz/ngai-tahu/creation-stories/the-settlement/claim-history/ ):
- 1849: "Ngāi Tahu made its first claim against the Crown for breach of contract in 1849."
- 1868: "When Ngāi Tahu first took its case to the courts, in 1868, the government passed laws to prevent the Courts from hearing or ruling on the case."
- 1986: "In 1986, Rakihia Tau filed the Ngāi Tahu claims with the Waitangi Tribunal." (name spelled as on the page)
- 1991: "Negotiations between the Crown and Ngāi Tahu on the claims began in 1991, after the release of the tribunal's Ngāi Tahu Land Claims report." Tribunal finding quoted there: "the Crown acted unconscionably and in repeated breach of the Treaty"

They led to the signing of the non-binding Heads of Agreement on the 5th of October 1996, then the signing of the Deed of Settlement at Kaikōura on the 21st November 1997, and the passage of the Ngāi Tahu Claim Settlement Act on the 29th September 1998."

## Corrections to carry into the game notes

1. Punting did not begin at the Antigua Boat Sheds. It began in February 1987 from the Thomas Edmonds Restaurant at the Edmonds band rotunda (search-excerpt evidence), stopped with the receivership in 1988 and was relaunched on 1 April 1989. The 1995 departure points are unproven.

3. SH1 past Christchurch: the old route ran through the city and out via Blenheim Road, Sockburn and Main South Road; the bypass via Harewood, the airport and Hornby replaced it "in the early 1990s". Sockburn is on the old route only.
4. Timaru SH1 in 1995: probably still Evans Street, Stafford Street, King Street. Theodosia Street and Craigie Avenue appear to have become SH1 by a 1996 Gazette notice. Needs the Gazette page.
5. The Bay Hill Piazza opened in 1997. Not present in 1995.
6. Timaru's lighthouse stood at Maori Park in 1995 (moved there 1980, moved again 2010).
7. Caroline Bay sound shell: 1937 or 1938, sources differ.
8. Rāpaki's carved wharenui Wheke dates from 2008. Not present in 1995.
9. Ngā Hau e Whā is described as in Bromley and is a pan-tribal urban marae, not a Ngāi Tahu rūnanga marae.
10. Timaru's Māori name: Kāti Huirapa prefer Te Maru according to RNZ's source; Te Tihi-o-Maru is a variant; the Ngāi Tahu atlas simply uses Timaru.
11. Temuka is Te Umu Kaha (three words). Orari is Ōrāri in the atlas. Whitebait is inaka. The 1996 magazine's words for elders are taua and poua.
12. Mid 1990s orthography (inference from 1996 documents): no macrons, "Ngai Tahu", "runanga", "mahinga kai"; k forms mainly in hapū and place names.
13. Ashburton lies where the Ngāi Tūāhuriri and Arowhenua takiwā descriptions meet or overlap (Rakaia to Hakatere).
14. South Island customary fishing regulations are 1998 and 1999, not 1995.
15. Negotiations were suspended by the Crown in August 1994 and resumed in 1996; the Te Rūnanga o Ngāi Tahu Act is 1996; the settlement Act is 1998.
